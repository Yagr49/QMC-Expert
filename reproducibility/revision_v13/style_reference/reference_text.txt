5) The authors used the organic molecules’ dataset to validate their models, including the descriptors. Why not use metal coordination compounds from the start, rather than only mentioning on page 12 a “minor loss of accuracy” when using both types of molecules? This “minor loss” should be quantified. The authors should provide sound arguments for initially leaving out the metal compounds to convince the reader (and me) that this approach is consistent and acceptable. 
We thank the reviewer for this important question. We would like to clarify that the organic dataset was used specifically for the descriptor selection and validation stage (Section "Validation of the descriptors", Figure 3), not for the final model evaluation. This choice was motivated by the fact that ML benchmarks for organic chromophores are well-established and reproducible across multiple independent studies (refs 10, 12, 41, 42), whereas the available ML results for metal-organic complexes are limited to specific subclasses (e.g., Ir(III) phosphors or Pt(II) emitters) with metrics that vary considerably depending on the metal center and ligand scaffold. The organic dataset therefore provides a more robust and universal baseline for validating the shared ligand-level descriptors (Morgan fingerprints, persistence barcodes) before extending the framework with metal-specific features. We have revised the text on page 10 to clarify this rationale and have quantified the accuracy trade-offs for joint training in the expanded Table 1 and on page 12: R² decreases from 0.946 to 0.814 (organic absorption) and from 0.860 to 0.789 (organic emission), while for metal-organic compounds R² slightly improves from 0.813 to 0.829 (absorption) and from 0.808 to 0.829 (emission).
6) “Note that the latter models trained with just one class of compounds typically handle the other class quite poorly, especially when the purely organic models are applied to the metalorganic complexes.” (page 12). This is not surprising, given the difficulties of ML in predicting properties (extrapolation) not included in the training dataset, and the approach the authors followed of initially training their models on a dataset with only one type of molecule. An additional comment regarding this point should be included in the text.
We agree with the reviewer that the poor cross-domain performance is expected from the standpoint of ML generalization. We have expanded the discussion on page 12 to provide a more detailed analysis of this phenomenon. Notably, the cross-domain failure is strongly asymmetric: organic-trained models applied to metal complexes yield R² = 0.012 (absorption) and 0.047 (emission), essentially equivalent to random prediction, whereas metal-organic-trained models applied to organic compounds still retain partial predictive power (R² = 0.626 and 0.616, respectively). This asymmetry is not merely a consequence of extrapolation beyond the training distribution but reflects a fundamental gap in the descriptor space: organic-trained models have never encountered informative Coulomb matrix features encoding the metal coordination environment, which SHAP analysis identifies as dominant for metal complex predictions. Conversely, metal-organic-trained models partially leverage the Morgan fingerprint and persistence barcode features learned from their ligand components, which share structural similarity with purely organic chromophores. This analysis further motivates the joint training approach adopted in our work, which directly addresses the descriptor gap by exposing the model to the full feature diversity of both compound classes.
7) More information can be extracted from the SHAP analysis – for instance, which features increase or decrease the target values (absorption and emission maxima). This information can be obtained from beeswarm plots.
We thank the reviewer for this valuable suggestion. We have generated beeswarm SHAP plots for all tasks (absorption, emission, emission with absorption data) separately for organic and metal-organic compounds. These plots, presented in the revised Figures S22- (Supporting Information) reveal the directionality of feature effects. For metal-organic compounds, high values of CM_0 (encoding the metal center nuclear charge) consistently shift predictions toward longer wavelengths, which is physically consistent with the stronger spin-orbit coupling and red-shifted MLCT bands of heavier metals. For organic compounds, Morgan fingerprint bits and persistence barcode features dominate, reflecting the role of specific molecular substructures in determining optical properties. The corresponding analysis has been added to the "Relative importance of features" section.
