1. Introduction

The antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a crucial challenge for modern society which is further exacerbated by nearly 40 years long period of “antibiotic discovery void” 1, 2. The WHO notes stagnant pipelines with 27 clinical agents against priorities in 2021 down from 31 in 2017, with 6 candidates fulfilling criteria for innovation 3. Overall, 13 new antimicrobials targeting priority pathogens were approved for the global market between 2017 and 2023 4. As of 2025 only 15 out of 90 clinical agents were innovative with just 5 targeting critical strains from WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List 5-7. Major shortages persist in oral outpatient therapies, pediatric formulations, and Gram-negative solutions, while preclinical volatility underscores need for sustained funding and innovation 7.

The twenty-first-century tool to boost innovation in antimicrobial pipelines is machine learning (ML) which is increasingly used to accelerate discovery, optimize efficacy–toxicity trade‑offs, and understand resistance mechanisms 8-17. Moreover, global trends indicate rapid ML incorporation in antimicrobial stewardship practices bringing innovative approaches to antimicrobial use optimization, personalized therapy and AMR reduction 18. There are now several case studies where ML has measurably shortened timelines for antimicrobial‑class discovery, including biocide‑related scaffolds and peptide‑based agents 19-21.

Pre-trained molecular models offer a reusable description of chemical structure, but adapting that description to a small antibacterial dataset still requires two choices: which information should guide the representation, and which parts of the prediction pipeline should be trained. Full backbone fine-tuning, low-rank updates inside transformer layers and an external feature encoder make different use of the available supervision. Their relative performance must therefore be tested under the intended validation regime, rather than inferred from the number of trainable parameters alone.

A particularly compelling strategy to overcome the limitations of purely data-driven approaches lies in the incorporation of domain knowledge and expert guidance into the modelling workflow. The concept, rooted in the physics-informed neural network (PINN) paradigm pioneered by Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis 22, posits that encoding structured prior knowledge into a learning algorithm constrains the space of admissible solutions, amplifying the information content of the data and enabling effective generalization even when training examples are scarce 22, 23. In the molecular sciences, this principle has been validated in multiple contexts: integrating substructure and physicochemical knowledge into deep learning architectures has been shown to improve molecular property prediction by up to 4% in regression and 13% in classification tasks 24; physics-informed graph neural networks have achieved thermodynamically consistent extrapolation in chemical systems 25; and biologically-informed neural network architectures that embed ontological relationships have demonstrated enhanced interpretability and performance in drug sensitivity prediction 26. The common thread across these advances is that expert-curated information—whether it takes the form of physical constraints, biological priors, or cross-domain annotations—can compensate for the limited size and diversity of chemical datasets, steering models toward physically and biologically plausible solutions.

A distinct and older line of work treats human expertise not as a constraint on the model but as a source of supervision in its own right. The intuition that experienced chemists possess a "gut feeling" for what constitutes a promising molecule has been formalised at least since QED, in which desirability functions were fitted to expert judgements of drug-likeness27. That intuition is known to be neither perfectly consistent nor bias-free — medicinal chemists reviewing the same compound sets agree only moderately with one another28, and their prioritisation carries systematic biases29— which is why expert labels are best aggregated across several annotators. Recent work has nonetheless shown that the signal survives aggregation and is machine-learnable: MolSkill distils the pairwise preferences of 35 medicinal chemists into an implicit scoring function that captures aspects of chemistry not covered by existing cheminformatic rule sets and outperforms QED at recognising drug-likeness30; in de novo design, fewer than 200 evaluations from a chemist are sufficient to reshape a generative model's objective31, and dedicated interfaces now exist for collecting such feedback at scale 32. The same economics of supervision is familiar outside chemistry, where reward models trained on binary human preferences steer large language models far more efficiently than dense supervision would 33, 34 [Christiano 2017; Ouyang 2022], and weak, noisy human heuristics can substitute for hand-labelled training data. What all of these approaches have in common is that human judgement is used to rank or filter candidate molecules. None of them asks whether human categorical judgement can replace quantitative supervision in learning the molecular representation itself, and none benchmarks it head-to-head, under an identical architecture, against a quantitative corpus an order of magnitude larger. That is the question we address here.

An extension of this idea that is particularly relevant to antimicrobial research is the transfer of knowledge between related biological tasks. Transfer learning and multi-task approaches, in which a model trained on one objective informs predictions on another, have proven effective in contexts ranging from protein language model fine-tuning for antimicrobial peptide classification 35 to BERT-based minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) regression for E. coli and S. aureus 36. In the more specific context of small-molecule antimicrobials, a stacking approach—wherein predictions from a model trained on one bacterial species are fed as features into a model for another—offers an elegant mechanism for cross-species information transfer. Such biology-informing strategies are grounded in the mechanistic insights such as non-specific membrane disruption, meaning that activity against one pathogen could encode relevant information to another. Expert annotation of these cross-domain relationships—identifying which experimental signals from one biological system carry predictive value for another—is therefore not merely a modelling convenience but a principled encoding of domain knowledge that can yield dramatic improvements in predictive accuracy.

The bottleneck in this domain, however, is not the choice of architecture but the nature of the data. Bioactivity corpora assembled from the literature are small, sparse, and — critically — protocol-heterogeneous. The problem compounds when values are pooled across laboratories: mixed IC₅₀ and Kᵢ data drawn from different sources carry an experimental uncertainty large enough to place a hard ceiling on the achievable accuracy of any model trained on them 37-39. The implication is uncomfortable but important: beyond a certain point, more quantitative data does not mean more information. This motivates a question that, to our knowledge, has not been asked directly — whether the fine quantitative gradation of activity is needed at all to learn a molecular representation, or whether a coarse categorical signal, provided by a human who has implicitly reconciled the incomparable protocols, suffices.

Our approach separates representation guidance from quantitative decoding. Frozen MolFormer first maps each molecular structure to a 768-dimensional vector. A compact external neural encoder then learns a 256-dimensional representation from expert Good/Bad judgements, while CatBoost learns the quantitative relationship between that representation and the MIC/MBC endpoints. Human labels guide source training; a new molecule does not require a human label when its representation or predicted activity is computed. This separation provides a concrete way to examine whether coarse expert supervision can organise useful molecular features for a distinct quantitative task.

In this paper, we will mainly focus on development and property predictions of cationic biocides, particularly membrane-targeting quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs), as antiseptics and disinfectants which are essential for prevention and treatment of microbial infections, especially healthcare-associated infections (HAIs). The main applications include variety of target objects from hand hygiene and surgical site preparation to surface cleaning providing rapid persistent antimicrobial action 40, 41. Previously, ML procedures were implemented in the chemistry workflow for antimicrobial activity estimation of cationic biocides derived from pyridine, cinchonine, imidazole, and bis-imidazole 42-48. Predictive ML model was developed for conjugated oligoelectrolytes utilizing domain-agnostic framework with a limited database of bis- and multi-QACs 49. Associative Neural Network was successfully employed to predict the MIC of ionic liquids 50, 51. Recent progress in QACs discovery was imbued by topology-aware generative model further highlighting practical validity of ML application 52.

In our previous work, we established a baseline framework for predicting QAC antibacterial activity using an MLP with MolFormer-derived embeddings to simultaneously predict MIC and MBC against Gram-positive S. aureus (ATCC 43300) and Gram-negative E. coli (ATCC 25922). A biology-informed stacking approach—transferring S. aureus predictions as input features to the E. coli model—nearly doubled R² for the Gram-negative pathogen. Validated on 27 out-of-domain QACs, the framework achieved 61–69% classification accuracy and R² = 0.68 on unseen scaffolds, while comparison with gradient boosting, Gaussian Processes, and GNNs showed that added architectural complexity did not improve accuracy.53. Building on this foundation, the present work pursues a different question: not how to engineer a better predictor, but where the predictive signal in such models comes from, and how human expertise can be used to shape it most economically. We make three contributions. First, we learn dedicated molecular embeddings by contrastive training on a compact set of expert “Good/Bad” activity classifications, and show that these 1,500 binary human judgements rival an order-of-magnitude larger corpus of roughly 10,000 quantitative measurements covering the same compound families, even surpassing it on the most difficult Gram-negative endpoint. Second, we show that when the prediction task carries a textual description of its classes—the bacterial species and the activity type—encoding that description with large-language-model text embeddings outperforms one-hot encoding, particularly under scarce data. Third, through an interpretable analysis combining representational similarity, feature attribution, and a controlled ablation, we demonstrate that the foundation model already encodes a rich description of these molecules and that human guidance serves to direct and expose its biologically relevant components for downstream predictors. We assess generalization on a held-out set of 17 newly synthesized QACs belonging to a previously unexplored structural subtype. Together, these results represent a step toward ML-controlled rational design of highly efficient biocidal agents, and toward a more economical division of labor between foundation-model knowledge and human expertise.

We also test whether a frozen representation followed by a decoder is preferable to direct backbone adaptation in this setting. The completed follow-up compares frozen prediction heads, LoRA and full fine-tuning, and evaluates both prediction routes from human-guided partial-backbone initialisation. Full fine-tuning improves cation-holdout prediction in the tested procedure but has higher error on the independently measured OOD compounds; freezing the human-guided partial-backbone representation and fitting CatBoost gives lower OOD error than direct prediction from the same source initialisations. These results motivate a conditional choice of prediction route, rather than a general claim that fine-tuning is harmful or that a smaller adapter is necessarily superior. Matched source panels and label-shuffling controls assess whether the assignment of supervisory labels contributes beyond an added training stage.

2.1 Training guidance NN models

Expert guidance datasets were constructed through systematic literature exploration followed by independent blind classification by three domain experts. Each reported compound was assigned an activity label ("Bad" or "Good") based on expert assessment of its antibacterial profile. A biocide agent was labeled "Good" based on objective correlation with its broad spectrum of antimicrobial activity. Additional criteria (e.g., cytotoxicity, cost, sustainability) were considered where possible. Disputed cases were resolved by majority among three experts. This procedure yielded a curated collection of 1,500 labeled compounds encompassing diverse antibacterial activity data.

The recovered computational archive contains 1,103 final molecule-level Good/Bad calls. To identify the optimal descriptor for downstream expert-guided learning, we benchmarked four molecular representations under a unified 5-fold stratified cross-validation protocol: transformer-based embeddings (MolFormer, 768-dimensional; ChemBERTa) and classical fingerprints (Morgan, MACCS). MolFormer embeddings consistently outperformed all alternatives, achieving the highest mean F1-score of 0.655 (MLP with StandardScaler) and accuracy of 0.856, whereas Morgan, MACCS, and ChemBERTa exhibited near-random F1 scores (≤0.50) despite moderate accuracy (Figure SI 1-2). This accuracy–F1 divergence indicates that classical fingerprints and the smaller chemical language model capture majority-class bias rather than biocide-specific structural motifs, whereas MolFormer’s pre-trained chemical vocabulary already encodes the subtle physicochemical nuances relevant to membrane-targeting cationics. Detailed per-representation and per-scaler comparisons are provided in the Supporting Information.

Building on the MolFormer baseline, we trained a dedicated MLP-based encoder (512 → 384 → 256 neurons) with a supervised contrastive objective to distill the implicit “chemical intuition” embedded in the expert labels into a compact, task-specific latent space. To address the inherent class imbalance of the expert-annotated dataset, we employed Borderline SMOTE (sampling strategy = 0.8) during encoder training. The model was optimized with a hard-negative contrastive loss, which explicitly pushes hard misclassified negatives away from the anchor embedding, thereby sharpening inter-class boundaries. The resulting 256-dimensional representations are referred to as human embeddings hereafter.

The reference encoder operates outside MolFormer. For a SMILES string s, the frozen backbone produces h₀ = fθ(s) ∈ R⁷⁶⁸. The archived StandardScaler normalises these input features before an external MLP produces z ∈ R²⁵⁶ through layers of width 512, 384 and 256. Expert labels train the MLP parameters ψ without updating the backbone parameters θ. A subsequent decoder predicts quantitative MIC/MBC from z and a task code. This feature adapter is distinct from LoRA updates inside the transformer and from partial or full backbone fine-tuning. The external encoder contains 691,584 trainable parameters. Its source-training contrastive projection head is removed when the 256-dimensional features are passed to the quantitative decoder.

Figure 2 presents t-SNE projections (perplexity = 30) of the held-out test fold before and after contrastive refinement. Prior to expert-guided training, active and inactive compounds were heavily intermingled (silhouette score = 0.007). After contrastive learning, the two classes resolved into distinct spatial regions, with the silhouette score rising to 0.108—a 15-fold improvement in cluster separability. This visual trend was corroborated by complementary internal validation metrics: the Davies–Bouldin index decreased from 6.54 to 2.58, while the Calinski–Harabasz score increased from 3.60 to 22.08 (Figure SI 4). Collectively, these metrics confirm that the encoder reorganized the latent space so that molecules with similar antibacterial behavior map to proximal regions.

The route comparison changes adaptation and decoding together and therefore does not isolate a causal effect of human-label meaning. To retain the compact v5 architecture when testing supervisory content, a separate controlled follow-up trained encoders on the same 681 source molecules (377 training and 304 validation), with target cations excluded and five paired source seeds. This follow-up fixes source exposure and training updates and omits SMOTE; it is not an exact replay of the archived SMOTE procedure. Human and shuffled-human guidance give OOD RMSE 2.268 ± 0.077 and 2.172 ± 0.112, respectively, where ± is sample SD over the five source runs. This compact-encoder control does not establish a human-content advantage. We consequently interpret the route results as evidence about the stated prediction procedures, without attributing them to a uniquely human semantic mechanism.
