Having obtained the embeddings, we next evaluated them on the MIC and MBC regression tasks for the 105 quaternary ammonium compounds introduced in our previous work. Each molecule is represented together with the bacterial species and activity type, allowing a shared CatBoost model to predict four quantitative endpoints. Expert Good/Bad labels train the representation; measured MIC/MBC values still train the downstream quantitative decoder. Complete reference results are provided in Tables S45–S46.

The original reference comparison shows the value of this task-aware formulation. With human-guided molecular features and text-encoded task descriptions, the recovered endpoint R² values are 0.75, 0.68, 0.59 and 0.77 for S. aureus MIC, S. aureus MBC, E. coli MIC and E. coli MBC, respectively. One-hot and categorical task coding provide simpler alternatives. Text descriptions are encoded with OpenAI text-embedding-3-small, giving one fixed 1,536-dimensional vector for each of the four species–endpoint combinations; exact inputs and cache provenance are provided in SI Section S34. The original comparison also reports close point estimates for the expert-guided and full-corpus quantitative encoders (Table S46), suggesting that coarse expert supervision can produce useful molecular features. Similar scores do not by themselves establish equivalence of the two sources or demonstrate a unique semantic benefit of text task descriptions.

To assess variability separately from these historical holdout results, we reconstructed five-fold row-wise cross-validation using the saved human-guided features and recovered decoder budget. Mean endpoint R² ranges from 0.449 to 0.717 with text task coding; complete mean ± fold SD values and raw-feature controls are in Table S40. This protocol predicts withheld task rows and may retain other measurements of the same molecule in training. Cation and scaffold holdouts address the different question of prediction for new molecular groups.

Figure 4 makes the supervision comparison explicit under a matched design. External compact encoders use the same 681 source molecules, identical source partitions and five paired seeds, with target cations excluded. Human labels train a contrastive encoder and continuous MIC values train a regression encoder; the backbone remains frozen. Independently fitted quantitative decoders use the same CatBoost budget and native categorical task codes. This no-SMOTE follow-up retains the v5 encoder architecture while controlling source exposure, rather than replaying the historical full-corpus quantitative checkpoint.

The resulting vertical violins show the observed endpoint-error distributions on the same 105 salts. Human and quantitative guidance give similar pooled errors, with their ordering depending on the holdout scheme. Shuffled-human guidance remains competitive in this compact-encoder experiment, limiting claims that human-label meaning uniquely explains the result. Detailed seed statistics and controls are in Table S41. These comparisons assess representation-learning procedures; an expert label is not assumed to be informationally equivalent to an individual concentration measurement.

Modern molecular and tabular models provide additional checks on the choice of decoder. Chemprop, TabPFN, TabFM and AutoGluon were evaluated on the same target registers. TabFM improves the separate-endpoint raw CatBoost pipeline in the grouped point comparisons, illustrating that the best procedure depends on both representation and decoder. Different PCA treatments and optimisation budgets make these practical pipeline comparisons. Full endpoint results and fold variability are provided in Tables S38 and S42.

We additionally tested transfer of source-trained representations to the independent Q-HERO activity-classification collection (Figure 5). After deduplication and exclusion of conflicting records, 405 molecules were classified by S. aureus MIC ≤8 µM using fixed five-fold scaffold holdouts. Source cations were excluded from this target cohort. Human-guided partial-backbone features with a newly trained CatBoost classifier give AUROC 0.675 ± 0.033, versus 0.595 for raw MolFormer and 0.565 for the authors’ RF architecture refitted on the same cohort and folds. Quantitative guidance gives 0.716 ± 0.010. These human and quantitative models use partial-backbone adaptation, distinct from the compact encoders in Figure 4; the external task therefore extends the supervision comparison across another representation-learning procedure.

On Q-HERO, human guidance also exceeds shuffled-human guidance (AUROC 0.567 ± 0.070). The paired difference is +0.108, with conditional 95% bootstrap interval [0.031, 0.237] over source/decoder seeds and scaffold clusters. The human−RF contrast is positive under scaffold evaluation (+0.110 [0.026, 0.201]), while its random-test interval includes zero [−0.066, 0.297]. This supports a contribution from label assignment in the stated procedure, without establishing a biological mechanism or superiority over quantitative guidance. The ± values denote sample SD of five complete runs. Since 360 of 405 molecules are active, precision–recall results are interpreted against the high positive-class prevalence. Full metrics, controls and provenance are in Tables S47–S48. The matched RF comparison concerns our cleaned cohort and scaffold protocol, rather than the authors’ original 421-row random test.
